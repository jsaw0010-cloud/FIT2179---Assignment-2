The Hidden Cost of Cheap Cigarettes

Malaysia taxes tobacco heavily — yet more than half of all cigarettes sold are smuggled, untaxed, and cheap. This is the story of how a well-intentioned sin tax created one of the world's largest illicit cigarette markets.

By Jim Saw Yong Jee  ·  May 2026  ·  Data: NHMS 2019, GATS-M 2023, ICS/Nielsen Malaysia

19% of Malaysian adults smoke — down from 23% in 2011
55% of cigarettes sold in Malaysia are illicit (2024)
RM 4–8 black market price vs RM 9–12 for a legal pack
42 sen excise duty per stick since Budget 2026
01 — Where

Smoking is not evenly spread across Malaysia

The burden of tobacco use falls heaviest on the northern and east coast states. Kedah leads at 27.6%, nearly double the rate in Putrajaya (12.3%). Rural states consistently show higher prevalence, reflecting income levels, enforcement gaps, and easier access to cheap contraband cigarettes. Fig. 1 — Smoking prevalence (%) by Malaysian state. Source: National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2019, Institute for Public Health Malaysia.

Key insight: Kedah's smoking rate of 27.6% is more than double that of Putrajaya (12.3%) — the urban-rural divide is the single strongest predictor of smoking prevalence across all Malaysian states.

Key findings

  • States above the national average (21.3%) are concentrated in the north and east coast
  • Urban federal territories (KL, Putrajaya) show the lowest rates, partly due to stricter enforcement
  • East Malaysia (Sabah 25.3%) bucks the rural trend due to high contraband inflow via sea borders

Now that we know where smoking is concentrated, let's see whether it has been declining over time.

02 — When

Smoking has been declining — but too slowly to meet targets

Malaysia set a national target of 15% smoking prevalence by 2025. The 2023 rate of 19% shows progress — four percentage points lower than 2011 — but the country remains well off track. At the current pace, the 15% target may not be reached until after 2030.

Fig. 2 — National smoking prevalence among adults aged 15+, 2011–2023. Sources: GATS 2011, NHMS 2015 & 2019, GATS-M 2023.

Key insight: At the current rate of decline, Malaysia will not reach its 15% target until after 2030 — five years late. The green dashed line shows exactly how far off track the country remains.

"The 2015–2016 tax hike was the single biggest push — but its gains were immediately undermined by the surge in contraband supply."

The decline is real — but what drove it? The answer lies in Malaysia's tobacco tax policy, and the story is more complicated than it first appears.

03 — The Tax

Excise duty was raised sharply in 2015–16, then frozen

For most of the 2000s, Malaysia taxed cigarettes at just 8–10 sen per stick. A dramatic restructuring in 2015 (to 20 sen) and 2016 (to 40 sen) more than quadrupled the rate in two years. Then came a seven-year freeze as the government grappled with political fallout from the illicit market boom. Budget 2026 finally added 2 sen more — a modest step.

Fig. 3 — Cigarette excise duty rate (sen per stick), 2004–2025. Source: Royal Malaysian Customs Department (RMCD) / Budget documents.

Key insight: The 2015–2016 hikes quadrupled the duty rate in just two years — the steepest tobacco tax increase in Malaysian history. The seven years of no change that followed reflect how politically difficult the issue became.

Higher taxes generated more government revenue — but that money came with an unexpected cost.

04 — The Revenue

Sin taxes generate billions — but tobacco dominates

Together, tobacco and alcohol excise duties contributed over RM 5.8 billion to government revenue by 2017. Tobacco alone accounts for roughly 75–80% of this total. While the revenue case for sin taxes is often cited by policymakers, alcohol revenue has barely grown — partly because Malaysia's Muslim-majority population already has low consumption rates.

Fig. 4 — Government excise revenue from tobacco and alcohol (MYR million), 2012–2017. Source: Galen Centre for Health and Social Policy / Ministry of Finance Malaysia.

Key insight: Tobacco contributes nearly 80% of all sin tax revenue; alcohol's share has barely grown despite rising duty rates — a reflection of Malaysia's predominantly Muslim demographics and cultural norms around alcohol consumption.

Revenue rose sharply after the tax hikes — but so did something else entirely. Something the government did not intend.

05 — The Unintended Consequence

Higher taxes fuelled a contraband explosion

The 2015–2016 tax hikes created an enormous price gap between legal and illegal cigarettes. Smugglers — mostly from duty-free zones in Labuan, Langkawi, and cross-border networks with Indonesia — flooded the market with cheap contraband. By 2020, nearly 2 in 3 cigarettes sold in Malaysia were illicit. Enforcement has since clawed back some ground, with the moratorium on new brands introduced in 2023 helping to stabilise the figure at 55%. Drag on the bottom mini-chart to zoom into any time period.

Fig. 5 — Illicit cigarette market share (%), 2015–2024. Source: Illicit Cigarette Study (ICS), Nielsen Malaysia / Confederation of Malaysian Tobacco Manufacturers.

Key insight: The illicit market nearly doubled in size between 2015 and 2020 — directly tracking the tax hikes year by year. Drag the bottom chart to zoom into the 2015–2018 surge and see the correlation clearly.

Why this matters

  • Illicit cigarettes bypass health warnings, age restrictions, and tar limits
  • Government loses an estimated RM 4–5 billion in foregone excise revenue annually
  • Low contraband prices directly undercut the tax's deterrent effect on smokers

How does Malaysia's situation compare to its neighbours? The regional picture puts the problem in sharp relief.

06 — Regional Context

Malaysia's legal cigarettes are cheap by ASEAN standards — except compared to contraband

A legal pack of cigarettes in Malaysia costs around USD 2.60 — far below Singapore's USD 13.50 but above Indonesia's USD 2.10. The critical issue is not the absolute price but the gap between legal and illicit. Malaysia's black market pack sells for USD 1.10 — less than half the legal price. No other ASEAN country has a comparable illicit market size.

Fig. 6 — Legal and illicit cigarette pack prices (USD) across ASEAN, 2023. Sources: WHO Tobacco Control Data, industry reports.

Key insight: Malaysia's illicit pack costs less than half the legal price — a gap no other ASEAN country comes close to matching. Singapore's high-tax approach ($13.50/pack) has suppressed both legal consumption and illicit trade through stringent border controls.

Across all these numbers, one question remains: who exactly is still smoking? The answer reveals deep social inequalities.

07 — Who Smokes

Smoking is overwhelmingly a male, lower-education phenomenon

Malaysia's smoking demographics reveal stark disparities. Men smoke at 35.7% — versus just 1.5% for women — one of the widest gender gaps in the world. Adults aged 25–44 have the highest rates, while those with only primary education smoke at three times the rate of university graduates. Rural residents smoke significantly more than urban dwellers. Use the dropdown to filter by demographic category.

Fig. 7 — Smoking prevalence (%) by demographic group. Sources: NHMS 2019, GATS-M 2023, Institute for Public Health Malaysia.

Key insight: Use the dropdown to filter — primary-educated adults smoke at 31.2%, nearly three times the rate of university graduates (10.8%). Education level is the strongest socioeconomic predictor of smoking in Malaysia.

The gender gap alone deserves closer examination — the divergence between men and women is one of the most striking patterns in Malaysia's smoking data.

08 — Gender Gap

Men and women smoke at vastly different rates

Malaysia has one of the widest gender gaps in smoking globally. Male smoking has fallen from 43.9% in 2011 to 35.7% in 2023, while female rates have barely moved, staying below 2% throughout. Social norms strongly discourage women from smoking in Malaysia, but this also means female smokers are less likely to seek cessation support or be counted in official surveys.

Fig. 8 — Male vs female smoking prevalence (%), 2011–2023. Sources: GATS 2011, NHMS 2015 & 2019, GATS-M 2023.

Key insight: Malaysia's gender gap in smoking (35.7% male vs 1.5% female) is one of the widest in the world. While male rates declined by 8 percentage points since 2011, female rates have barely shifted — suggesting different social dynamics govern each group.

The gap is also visible across ethnic communities — smoking rates differ significantly depending on cultural background.

09 — Ethnicity

Smoking rates vary significantly across ethnic groups

Malay and other Bumiputera communities show the highest smoking rates, while Chinese and Indian Malaysians smoke considerably less. This pattern is consistent across all NHMS surveys since 2011 and likely reflects a combination of cultural norms, income levels, and geographic distribution across urban and rural areas.

Fig. 9 — Smoking prevalence (%) by ethnicity, 2019. Source: National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2019.

Key insight: Malay smokers (22.6%) outnumber Indian smokers (11.5%) by nearly two to one — a disparity consistent across all NHMS surveys since 2011. Targeted cessation programmes by ethnicity could be more effective than blanket tax policies.

With all demographic patterns established, the final chart brings the central paradox into sharp focus — overlaying the tax rate directly against the illicit market share.

10 — The Paradox

Every tax hike drove more smokers to the black market

When excise duty rose steeply in 2015 and 2016, the illicit market share shot up almost in lockstep. This chart overlays both trends — the red line tracks illicit share (left axis), the blue dashed line tracks duty rate (right axis). The direct relationship between higher tax rates and black market growth is unmistakable. It raises a difficult policy question: can Malaysia keep raising tobacco taxes without simply enriching smugglers?

Fig. 10 — Excise duty (sen/stick) vs illicit market share (%), 2015–2024. Sources: RMCD; ICS/Nielsen Malaysia.

Key insight: The correlation is striking — every time duty rose, the illicit share rose within 12 months. The left axis (red) shows illicit market share; the right axis (blue dashed) shows excise duty. Both move in the same direction.

The policy dilemma

  • Higher taxes reduce legal sales but do not necessarily reduce total consumption
  • Smuggled cigarettes undercut the tax's health deterrent entirely
  • Enforcement, not just taxation, is needed to close the gap
